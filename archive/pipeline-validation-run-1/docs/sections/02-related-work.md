## Data-driven fouling prediction

Recent fouling-prediction studies establish a data-driven baseline family but do not, on the accessible evidence, answer the controlled extrapolation question posed here. The abstract of @he2025machine reports four neural-network surrogates trained on CFD-generated fouling data for a shell-and-tube steam-generator case, with noise sensitivity used to assess robustness. CFD supplies the data, rather than an explicit fouling correlation embedded in the learned predictor. The abstract does not report an interpolation-versus-extrapolation split; because only the abstract was available, this is not an absence claim about the full paper. Similarly, the abstract of @hosseini2022novel compares Gaussian-process regression, decision trees, bagged trees, support-vector regression, and linear regression for estimating heat-exchanger fouling factor from operating and construction variables, and reports Gaussian-process regression as the strongest method. Its abstract mentions neither an embedded physics correlation nor an extrapolation split, but no corresponding claim is made about unexamined full-text content. These studies motivate a black-box comparator and confirm that fouling-related surrogate prediction is an active application, while leaving architecture-controlled out-of-envelope evidence unresolved.

## Mechanistic correlations, simulators, and omitted mechanisms

Mechanistic fouling work spans compact correlations and whole-exchanger models, which serve different roles in the present design. The full text of @wilson2017twenty documents the Ebert–Panchal threshold equation as a net fouling rate with a Reynolds-number- and temperature-dependent deposition term opposed by a wall-shear suppression term. That source, rather than a whole-model paper, is the basis for treating Ebert–Panchal as the published closed-form correlation proposed for the hybrid's fixed physics component. The full text of @yang2020computational separately studies the induction period of crude-oil fouling and distinguishes reaction-driven and precipitation-driven mechanisms. An induction-state mechanism is absent from the documented Ebert–Panchal threshold form, providing an independently sourced candidate discrepancy for a simulator to include.

This division of evidence does not establish C0. It supports only the design premise that an independently selected published correlation and a separately documented omitted mechanism can be paired. Whether the implemented simulator actually contains that mechanism, whether the selected Ebert–Panchal parameters are transcribed and converted correctly, and whether the residual-free correlation leaves error above the preregistered noise threshold remain future implementation and experimental gates. In particular, numerical coefficients extracted from the source must be visually verified with their equation, table, units, and variant before use.

The abstracts of @coletti2011dynamic and @dazbejarano2016new provide broader whole-model context: respectively, a dynamic distributed shell-and-tube crude-oil fouling model and a reactive-deposit model applied to fouling–cleaning cycles. They support the plausibility of dynamic mechanistic simulation, not the claim that either is the embedded closed-form correlation. At abstract level, @deshannavar2021revisiting also supplies limitation context for threshold models, including concerns about how temperature and velocity effects are represented. None of these abstract-only sources establishes the simulator–correlation discrepancy required by C0.

## Physics-informed and hybrid architectures

Physics can enter learning through different architectural commitments. @bradley2022perspectives distinguishes hybrid submodeling, in which physical and data-driven equations are combined as model components, from physics-informed machine learning that incorporates governing knowledge through training or representation. This distinction matters because an additive prediction consisting of a fixed mechanistic term plus a learned discrepancy is not equivalent to imposing a differential-equation residual in the loss. At abstract level, @azadi2022hybrid provides a parallel example outside fouling: a first-principles blast-furnace model is combined with a data-based model that compensates for mechanistic deficiencies. It is used here only as evidence that this parallel compensation structure exists across process modeling, not as extrapolation evidence.

A heat-exchanger-adjacent loss-penalty example is @jadhav2022physics. For a rotary air preheater, it penalizes violations of governing heat-transfer PDEs, boundary conditions, and interfaces while learning a temperature field. Fouling propensity is then computed separately from the predicted temperatures rather than learned as the network's direct target. The study compares the PINN with a finite-difference solver and compares training from scratch with transfer learning, but provides no purely data-driven baseline for the temperature-field task. Its boundary-condition cases receive separate transfer-learned models, so it does not implement a held-out design in which one fitted model extrapolates to unseen operating conditions. It therefore demonstrates the loss-penalty family without supplying the residual-hybrid comparison required for C2. At abstract level, @bonfanti2024generalization further shows that generalization outside the training domain and its hyperparameter dependence are studied for PINNs; it is not evidence for a direct additive-residual-versus-black-box comparison.

An Ebert–Panchal ODE loss penalty is nevertheless a feasible alternative: a network could be trained against the residual of the published rate equation. It is deliberately not taken here, not because capacity matching would be harder, but because it would estimate soft ODE enforcement under a penalty-weighted objective, require selection of a penalty weight, and would not provide the same removable learned correction used by C0's residual-free falsifier. The choice is thus about the estimand and its falsifiability, not an expectation that additive residuals are superior.

## Extrapolation evidence and novelty boundary

The closest direct precedent is @gallup2023physics. In a continuous stirred-tank reactor study, models were trained over inlet temperatures of 400–420 °C and tested out of range at 430–450 °C. Its architecture-plus-conjunction model, a residual-physics analog to the proposed additive hybrid, produced 4.50% out-of-range error versus 6.38% for the black-box and was described as only slightly better. A loss-penalty model achieved 2.28%, substantially outperforming both; this negative precedent for the present architectural choice is retained rather than discounted.

The comparison does not isolate architectural effect. The hybrid used hidden layers with 5 nodes, whereas the black-box baseline used six layers of 20, and the paper reports neither a paired repeated-seed design nor a paired statistical test. The capacity difference is therefore a confound, but its direction is unknown: smaller capacity cannot be assumed either to help or harm extrapolation. Consequently, @gallup2023physics threatens the hypothesized C2 outcome while leaving open whether a controlled comparison would reproduce it. C2 remains a prospective, contingent hypothesis, not a literature-supported result, and no superiority claim follows from the proposed design.

The exact novelty boundary is a controlled matched-capacity, identical-search-budget, repeated-seed, paired-test extrapolation comparison for synthetic shell-and-tube fouling resistance.
